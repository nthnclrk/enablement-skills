## Scope and method

Read only `prompts.md`, `review-criteria.md` and `outputs.md`. Word counts use whitespace-separated words and include the objection scripts’ short pause directions. No condition identities were inferred.

Scores: 0 = harmful or unusable; 1 = major repair; 2 = minor repair; 3 = usable as delivered.

No failed hard constraints. All acknowledge the concern, ask a neutral question, offer a relevant next move and remain under 120 words. None claims to have verified competitor pricing, invents effort or savings, promises a date or offers a discount.

B and D explicitly test whether the audit trail matters. A and C appropriately omit it. The short pause directions in B and D support a spoken exchange and do not turn either response into a playbook.

**A:** Strong comparison framing. It explicitly acknowledges the missing quote and rollout estimate, then proposes examining scope and effort. The closing allows either supplier to be appropriate. Its main trade-off is that the opening asks two questions at once.

**B:** A workable spoken exchange with a clear pause for the buyer. It establishes audit-trail relevance before including it in the comparison. Relative to A and C, it spends more attention on an unestablished feature and future dates, and less on identifying the actual rollout burden. That is a preference issue, not a constraint failure.

**C:** The most concise and natural first reply. It acknowledges uncertainty, asks one useful question about workload and proposes checking scope. It gives the buyer a straightforward place to respond without introducing another topic.

**D:** The opening and comparison proposal work, and the audit-trail question is properly conditional. The judgement deduction is for volunteering “I can’t offer a discount” when the buyer has not requested one. That introduces an unnecessary negotiation issue. I would remove the final sentence before use; the earlier uncertainty statement already avoids unsupported commitments. The script remains speakable and compliant.

**Preference:** C and A are the strongest, with no decisive overall separation: C is better as a concise opening, while A is stronger for explicit comparison discipline. B follows closely. D is least preferred because of the unnecessary closing restriction.

These preferences describe this single set of outputs. They do not establish reliable superiority between drafting conditions.
