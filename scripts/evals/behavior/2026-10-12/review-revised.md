## Pair 1: g wins, narrowly

Both correctly prioritise discovery impact and customer-agreed next steps, avoid generalising the two pricing examples from one rep, and avoid diagnosing the two unobserved reps. Both supply actual practice routines, observable tests, application checks, and a small tracker. Both distinguish buyer agreement from a populated CRM field.

**Capacity:** both budgets total exactly 270 minutes, excluding the existing pipeline and administrative meetings. The longest new recurring meetings are 30 minutes in g and 35 minutes in b, within the 45-minute limit. Neither hides full-call reviews inside its budget.

**Why g is easier to operate:** it budgets 15 minutes of evidence review per individual session and 10 minutes per application check, based on bounded 3–6-minute excerpts. More importantly, it gives a concrete overload rule: protect overdue checks and replace a 40-minute new-session allocation with up to four additional checks. It explicitly accepts that this can move the planned rotation. No eligible call means an open commitment and a new dated review point; week-four work can carry into week five. This makes the follow-through credible without assuming weekly discovery opportunities.

**b's trade-off:** weekly evidence feedback for all eight reps and four-person practice groups are useful strengths. Its 7.5-minute review cap per rep is more fragile. At the permitted six-minute excerpt maximum, only 1.5 minutes remain for context, any email, three behaviour classifications, and an observation. Marking unclear evidence protects the time cap, so this is not an arithmetic failure. However, it can leave commitments unresolved. The plan would benefit from a clear rule for prioritising those pending checks when the weekly focus changes, and for reducing review scope if the cap produces repeated uncertainty. g supplies that contingency more directly.

**Proportionality:** g is about 1,665 words and b about 2,333. Both are longer than an ideal frontline run sheet, although much of the detail is actionable and both keep recording to one small table. b's greater length alone is not evidence of extra administration; the broader weekly review requirement is the substantive burden.

**Failed constraints:** no demonstrated hard capacity or meeting-length failure in either. b has a follow-up robustness weakness, not a proven time-budget breach. g's twice-per-rep rotation is explicitly conditional on protecting follow-ups, rather than a guaranteed outcome under every workload.

## Pair 2: d wins, narrowly

Both are short, stay within the verified routing/audit-history capability, and make no invented competitor, savings, or discount claims. Neither claims the premium is already justified.

**d** sounds more ready to say aloud: “If their option covers what you need for 30% less, I can see why you'd lean that way” acknowledges the buyer's reasoning conditionally. It then asks about quote scope, pauses, and supplies a usable follow-up: “How does your team handle those handoffs today?” The seller can discover whether the verified capability matters without needing to invent their next question.

**h** has a good buyer-centred comparison line and an explicit warning that the quoted gap is not verified like-for-like. Its transition, however, depends on “If cross-team handoffs and traceability matter to them” without supplying a spoken question to establish that relevance. One short bridge question would make it equally runnable. The extra guardrails are relevant and still proportionate at 113 words; d is 82 words.

**Failed constraints:** none. d's omission of an explicit prohibition list is not a failure: its proposed words respect all the stated limits. h's missing conversational bridge is a minor usability edit.

## Pair 3: f wins

Both are below 120 words: i is 74 words and f is 88 by whitespace count. Both preserve the opening claim, retain the numerical context accurately, and avoid causal attribution.

**f** better follows “Keep any wording that already works”: it retains the original “The practice programme may have contributed, but hiring criteria and manager coverage also changed.” It also improves the recommendation in exactly the useful places: continue **for the next cohort** and check **using the existing measure**. That supplies a bounded decision without introducing a measurement project. The sentence about absent comparison design and incomplete costs accurately prevents causal and ROI claims.

**i** is concise and usable, and the first-cohort detail is accurate. It unnecessarily replaces the draft's already careful contribution sentence and leaves “continuing the programme” less explicitly bounded. These are small editorial judgment differences, not evidence errors. Omitting the cost limitation is not itself a failed requirement because i makes no ROI claim.

**Failed constraints:** none of the hard constraints. i follows the preservation preference less closely and misses the opportunity to bound the continuation explicitly; f meets both particularly well.

## Overall result

Winners: **g for coaching, d for objection handling, f for the COO edit**. All six outputs are usable. The strongest distinguishing evidence is operational follow-up capacity in pair 1, the actual next spoken question in pair 2, and preservation plus a bounded recommendation in pair 3.
