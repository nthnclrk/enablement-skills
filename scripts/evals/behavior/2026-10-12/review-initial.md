## Pair A/B: four-week manager coaching rhythm

**Winner: B, narrowly. Both are credible, runnable plans.**

Both correctly prioritise discovery impact and next-step quality over the manager's pricing hypothesis. Both preserve the limitations of the 12-call sample, avoid labelling the two unsampled AEs, distinguish CRM hygiene from buyer-agreed next steps, and avoid revenue or readiness claims. Both budgets add to 270 minutes and keep every new meeting below the 45-minute ceiling. Each includes evidence review, rehearsal, a live commitment and follow-up rather than merely scheduling meetings.

**Why B edges ahead:** its operating loop is more consistent across all eight reps. It promises “weekly practice and evidence feedback, plus two individual sessions across the month”, then shows how those activities fit the budget. Its next-eligible-call selection, explicit observable tests, and retained weekly observations make the loop easy to continue. “Choose the next eligible call, not the rep's best call” is a useful protection against cherry-picking. The distinction between a rep's question and the buyer's answer is also strong coaching judgment. The group agenda guarantees each rep a turn, specific feedback and a retry.

**A's strengths:** it offers more generous individual preparation and rehearsal, prioritises the two missing baselines explicitly, includes the rep's correction of the evidence, and has the stronger capacity contingency: “run three individual cycles that week” if evidence takes longer. Its protection of individual coaching records is more explicit than B's.

**A's usability deduction:** the manager must reconcile follow-up demand across alternating groups. Week two asks for Group A checks and Group B checks as evidence becomes available, but there are only four ten-minute check slots. A explicitly acknowledges this and prohibits invisible carry-over work, so this is not a false-budget claim. It would still benefit from a simple priority rule for outstanding versus new checks and a clear way to show the resulting rescheduled cycle. The final formal-performance escalation paragraph also expands beyond what is needed to launch this developmental rhythm.

**B's remaining trade-offs:** reviewing up to six minutes of excerpts, their context, a possible email, three behaviours and a feedback observation in 7.5 minutes is tight. B handles uncertainty honestly rather than instructing the manager to overrun, but this cap can leave more evidence unresolved. The two requested excerpts concern impact and the close; objection diagnosis should only be coded when an objection is actually present in the reviewed material. “Private team document” also needs care: appropriate access should mean that individual coaching notes are not automatically visible to the whole team.

**Constraint findings:** no clear hard constraint failed. Both respect the time and meeting limits and provide low-admin mechanisms. Both need a shorter first-use view: the issue is finding this week's actions among the extensive guidance, not word count itself.

**Improve both:** put the weekly budget, group allocation, next commitment and check queue into a compact run sheet. Keep the longer agendas as supporting guidance. Make access to individual rows explicit, and name the rule for handling evidence that cannot be assessed within the allocated time. B would benefit from A's capacity contingency; A would benefit from B's simpler, recurring evidence loop.

## Pair C/D: immediate response to a cheaper competitor

**Winner: D, narrowly, for this particular brief.**

Both stay within the verified capability. Neither invents savings, asserts that the competitor lacks routing or audit history, offers a discount, or declares the 30% comparison like-for-like.

**C has stronger diagnostic coverage.** It separates value uncertainty from a firm budget ceiling and offers an appropriate stopping point when the price cannot fit. Its line “I don't yet know what's included in their quote, so I can't tell you whether that's a difference” is exemplary evidence discipline. The proposed next step—compare a real handoff and the quotes—is commercially useful.

**D is more immediately usable.** It starts with a short acknowledgement, asks what the quote includes, pauses, and then asks “How does your team handle those handoffs today?” That is a natural conversational sequence for a call in 30 minutes. Its closing guidance correctly says there is not yet enough evidence to justify the premium. It is useful without requiring the seller to learn several branches.

**C's deductions:** “have you just not seen enough difference” can sound slightly dismissive, and the value response combines a capability statement, an evidence caveat, a relevance question and an evaluation proposal in one long turn. The written safeguards are sound; the spoken material needs shortening. At 196 words, this is not literally a whole playbook, but it is more scaffolding than the user needs.

**D's domain deduction:** it opens the conversation well but leaves the next commercial step implicit. If the handoff problem matters, the seller still needs to agree how to assess it against the competing quote. That is a small omission, not a reason to add a full playbook.

**Constraint findings:** no hard factual or commercial constraint failed. C is a weaker fit for the natural, minimal-response preference; D is a weaker guide to what happens after the buyer answers.

**Improve both:** retain D's short turns and add only a conditional next step: “If that matters, could we compare one real handoff and what's included in each quote?” C should separate its questions and replace “have you just not seen” with neutral wording. D could keep C's budget-versus-value question in reserve if the buyer remains focused on price.

## Pair E/F: COO update, maximum 120 words

**Winner: F.**

Both meet the word limit: E is 83 words and F is 88 by whitespace count. Both accurately report the cohort sizes, medians, nine-day difference and consistent definition. Neither attributes the improvement to the programme, claims ROI or turns the recommendation into a measurement project.

**F is the closer edit of an already sound draft.** It preserves “The practice programme may have contributed, but hiring criteria and manager coverage also changed.” Its recommendation is bounded and operational: “continuing the programme for the next cohort” and checking the result “using the existing measure.” That makes the next decision clear without adding a new workstream.

**E is also usable.** Its “about an 18% reduction” is mathematically correct, and it usefully notes that this was the first programme cohort. However, the extra derived statistic adds little to the already clear nine-day result. E also replaces more of the working causal-language sentence than necessary. These are proportionality and editing-fidelity issues, not factual errors.

**Constraint findings:** no hard constraint failed. F meets the preserve-working-wording preference more closely. Both keep the recommendation clear and proportionate.

**Improve both:** put the recommendation first if the COO prefers decision-first updates. Otherwise F can be used as delivered; E needs only removal of the non-essential percentage and a more bounded continuation recommendation. Neither needs extra metrics, tests or a research plan.

## Overall finding

The differences are modest, and all six outputs are safe and useful. B wins on a repeatable all-rep coaching loop, D on immediate conversational use, and F on disciplined editing and a bounded recommendation. None wins because it is simply longer or shorter; each winner better matches the work the recipient must do next.
